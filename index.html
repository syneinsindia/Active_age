Heart & Breath Signal — Web Spike v—


Engineering feasibility spike for the ratified Heart & Breath Signal check-in (§4A) — camera-PPG resting heart rate, HRV, and respiratory rate from a fingertip-on-back-camera scan. Not the production feature, not a diagnosis: plain readings only, exactly like the shipped design. This page checks whether a browser can extract a usable signal this way at all. (Front camera + screen backlight was tried first and dropped — weaker/less steady light gave much noisier results, and on some Android phones the front camera couldn't be used for this at all.)




How to run this (read once)

    

  1. Find somewhere to sit still and rest your hand — ideally prop the phone on a table rather than holding it free-hand. Motion and pressure changes are the #1 thing that ruins this signal.
    2. 

  2. Turn your phone's flashlight on yourself first (Control Center → flashlight icon on iPhone) — the page can't switch it on for you. Leave it on for the whole session.
    3. 

  3. Tap Start Camera, then gently but fully cover both the flash and the rear camera lens with your fingertip — enough to block them completely, not so hard it goes white/loses circulation. The preview should turn a deep red once you're covering it correctly — that's expected, that's the light passing through your finger.
    4. 

  4. Tap Start Capture. Keep your hand and finger completely still and don't talk for the full window (30–60s). Watch the Contact steadiness meter — it tells you in real time if your pressure or position needs adjusting, well before the capture ends.
    5. 

  5. When it finishes, you'll get Heart Rate, HRV, and Respiratory Rate. If you have a reference (a manual pulse count, a smartwatch, a pulse oximeter) type it into that trial's row for comparison.
    6. 

  6. Run a few captures, export CSV and send it back with any notes.
    7. 



For reference from the literature: independently-validated camera-PPG heart rate typically runs about 2–4.5 bpm mean error against ECG — useful context for judging whether a given reading is in a reasonable range, not a pass/fail line.

Range of Motion — Web Spike v—


Engineering feasibility spike for the ratified Range of Motion test — Ankle Flex, Knee Bend, Hip Raise, and Arm Raise (clinically: ankle dorsiflexion, knee flexion, hip flexion, shoulder flexion/abduction) — MediaPipe pose landmarks in the browser used as a goniometer. Not the production feature, not a diagnosis: this page only checks whether a phone camera can extract a usable joint-angle reading this way at all.




How to run this (read once)

    

  1. Pick the movement and which side (left/right) below — the setup note and the little animated diagram change to match. Each movement has its own simple picture showing the motion.
    2. 

  2. Prop the phone up (against a wall, box, or have a helper hold it) so your whole limb stays in frame for the whole movement — handheld-by-the-mover doesn't work here.
    3. 

  3. Tap Start Camera. You'll see a skeleton overlay and a dashed "stand here" line near the bottom of the view — the two thick green highlighted segments and the joint between them are what's being measured. Reposition until the "Tracking" status says tracking well.
    4. 

  4. Tap Start Capture — you'll then get a few seconds (shown on screen, adjustable below) to walk to the marked line and settle into your relaxed starting position. Once that countdown ends, hold still for 2 seconds (this sets the baseline). Then, when it says "move & hold end range", move to your fullest comfortable range and hold it until the capture ends.
    5. 

  5. You'll get a Baseline angle, a Peak angle, and the ROM (the difference) in degrees. If a helper measured the same end-range position with a real goniometer, type that into the trial's Reference column.
    6. 

  6. Run a few trials, export CSV, and send it back with any notes.
    7. 



This reads the raw geometric angle between three tracked body points (e.g. knee–ankle–forefoot for the ankle) — it isn't calibrated to a specific clinical zero yet. The point of this spike is to see how consistently it tracks and how close it lands to a real goniometer reading, not to produce a clinically calibrated number.

Breath Blast — Web Spike v—


Engineering feasibility spike for a microphone-based breath / lung-capacity check — blow steadily into the phone's mic and see what the captured loudness envelope actually looks like. Not the production feature, not a diagnosis, and explicitly not a spirometer reading — see the note below. This page only exists to answer "what does a real capture look like" (§13 of the digital-vital-signs feasibility doc).




What this measures — and what it doesn't. This spike reports the shape and timing of your exhale blast as heard by the microphone: how long the blow lasted (forced expiratory time), how quickly it hit its loudest point, and a relative loudness number. It does not report FEV1, FVC, PEF, or any volume in litres — the published mic-based spirometry research (SpiroSmart, SpiroCall) only reaches usable accuracy on those numbers with extra calibration steps this spike doesn't have, and no mic-only spirometer has FDA clearance. Treat every number below as a relative, uncalibrated engineering measurement, not a medical reading.





How to run this (read once)

    

  1. Find a reasonably quiet room — background noise (fans, traffic, other people talking) is the main thing that ruins this capture.
    2. 

  2. Tap Start Microphone and allow mic access. Hold the phone about 15–20cm (6–8 inches) from your mouth — off to the side slightly is fine, straight at your mouth can clip/distort on a hard blow.
    3. 

  3. Watch the background level meter settle — it should read low and steady before you continue. If it's jumpy, find a quieter spot.
    4. 

  4. Tap Start Capture. After the short "get ready" countdown, take a full breath in, then blow out steadily and completely into the mic in one continuous breath — like fogging a mirror, not a short puff.
    5. 

  5. The capture stops on its own after the set duration. You'll get forced expiratory time (FET), time-to-peak, and a relative peak-loudness number, plus a picture of the actual blast shape.
    6. 

  6. Run a few captures, export CSV and send it back with any notes. Leave the capture duration at 15s or more unless you know you're a short blower — real test rounds found blows lasting well past 10 seconds, and shortening the window cuts off the tail of a long one (flagged as "truncated" in the notes when it happens).
    7. 



For reference from the literature: even the calibrated mic-based spirometry research (SpiroSmart/SpiroCall, phone mic + a printed or 3D-printed mouthpiece funnel) runs roughly 4–8% mean error against a real spirometer on FEV1/FVC — and that's with a funnel and per-device calibration. This spike has neither, so it deliberately doesn't attempt those numbers at all.

Heart Rate — SQI+FFT v—


Experimental alternative analysis pipeline for the same camera-PPG capture as the original Heart & Breath Signal spike — same finger-on-rear-camera-plus-flashlight setup, different math. Built to test whether splitting the capture into short segments, keeping only the segments with a clean spectral signal, and reading heart rate as a frequency (FFT/Goertzel) rather than by counting individual time-domain peaks holds up better when a hand can't stay perfectly still for the full window. Not the production feature, not a diagnosis.




Why this exists: testers across a range of ages found it hard to hold a hand steady on the camera for the original tool's full 30–60s window, and any shaky stretch could ruin the whole reading. This version tolerates that: it keeps only the segments where the signal was actually clean and reports from those, instead of failing the whole capture over one bad moment. Run a few captures with both tools back to back and compare which one behaves better with real hands.





How to run this (read once)

    

  1. Same physical setup as the original tool: prop the phone on a table or brace your forearm rather than holding it free-hand if you can — this still helps, this tool just tolerates it less perfectly than that.
    2. 

  2. Turn your phone's flashlight on yourself first (Control Center → flashlight icon on iPhone) — the page can't switch it on for you. Leave it on for the whole session.
    3. 

  3. Tap Start Camera, then gently but fully cover both the flash and the rear camera lens with your fingertip. The preview should turn a deep red once you're covering it correctly.
    4. 

  4. Tap Start Capture. The capture window is shorter than the original tool's by default (25s) since this method doesn't need as long — try the default first. Watch the segment strip below the waveform: each block is a ~5s slice as it's analyzed, colored green (used) or orange (excluded) live as the capture runs.
    5. 

  5. When it finishes you'll get a heart rate reading plus how many of the segments were actually used. If you have a reference (manual pulse count, smartwatch, oximeter) type it into that trial's row.
    6. 

  6. Run a few captures, export CSV and send it back with any notes — ideally alongside a few runs of the original tool on the same person for comparison.
    7. 



HRV is not reported by this pipeline (RMSSD needs individually-timed beats, which this frequency-domain method doesn't produce) — use the original tool if HRV is what you're comparing. Respiratory rate reuses the same slow-baseline-wander method as the original tool unchanged.

Cardio Response Test


A metronome-paced marching bout at a fixed, repeatable cadence, then one heart-rate and breathing-rate reading right after — in the mold of submaximal step tests like the YMCA 3-Minute Step Test. The point is a standardized, repeatable effort so your response to it is comparable session to session — not a one-off recovery curve (that's the separate Heart Recovery Speed test). No VO2max estimate, no fitness score: just how your heart rate and breathing responded to this session's effort, and its trend over time.




  Before you start: this involves marching in place at a set pace for 2-4 minutes. Only do this if you're already comfortable with that level of activity — stop immediately if you feel dizzy, unusually short of breath, or unwell. Hold onto a chair or wall if steadiness is a concern; march seated (lifting knees) if standing isn't comfortable.

Chest Breathing Scan


A second, non-contact way to read respiratory rate — the camera watches the chest rise and fall instead of reading it from a fingertip pulse signal. No finger on the lens, no flashlight. Best used propped on a stand or table, not hand-held.




  Untested against real captures. Walking Endurance


A longer timed walk, tracked by the phone's motion sensor while carried in a pocket or held — a different measure than the short Gait Speed test: sustained walking capacity over minutes, not usual walking speed over a few metres.




  Steps and time only — deliberately no distance shown. Estimating distance from a phone's motion sensor alone needs a stride-length calibration that hasn't been validated for this population yet, and a wrong distance number would look more precise than it is. Step count and elapsed time are both measured directly and shown as-is.

Single-Leg Balance


How long you can hold your balance standing on one leg — eyes open, then eyes closed. Camera-tracked: watches your arms, raised foot, stance foot, and (on eyes-closed trials) your eyes, and stops the timer automatically when any of them breaks position.




  Use a phone's back camera, not a laptop webcam. A webcam's wide-angle lens can't frame a full body cleanly at normal desk distance — you either get cropped or distorted. Prop the phone against something stable (a shelf, a stack of books), back camera facing you, far enough back that your whole body is in frame — head to feet. Match the "FEET HERE" line on screen while you're still close enough to check it, then step back into position. Bare feet, hands on hips or arms crossed over your chest, raised foot held near (not touching) your stance-leg ankle. Tapping Start Trial begins a short voice-guided count-in — the camera checks you're on the leg picked below during that count-in and while calibrating, and speaks a correction ("wrong leg") if it isn't, restarting the count-in automatically. This is a first-pass automatic build — if the camera doesn't catch it, tap "I lost balance" yourself; that always works as a backup, and every trial records which of the two ended it.

Sit-to-Stand Performance


Stand up and sit back down as many times as you safely can in 30 seconds — the standard 30-second chair-stand test (same protocol family as the CDC STEADI Chair Stand). Camera-tracked: counts each full stand-sit cycle automatically by watching your hip height.




  Use a phone's back camera, not a laptop webcam. A webcam's wide-angle lens can't frame a full sit-to-stand cleanly at normal desk distance — you either get cropped or distorted. Prop the phone against something stable, back camera facing you, far enough back that you're fully in frame both sitting and standing — match the "CHAIR HERE" line on screen while you're still close enough to check it, then set the chair there. Use a sturdy, armless chair against a wall so it can't slide. Arms crossed over your chest, feet flat. Stop immediately if you feel unsteady. This is a first-pass automatic count — check it against the live number and use the +/− buttons to correct it if the camera misses a rep or double-counts one.

Finger Tapping


Tap the button as fast and steadily as you comfortably can for 10 seconds — a simple coordination game, the same idea as drumming your fingers on a table.




  Use one finger on your preferred hand, and keep tapping the same spot. There's no "right" score here — this just captures your tap count and rhythm as a baseline to compare against your own future checks. Not a test of reflexes, memory, or coordination disorders — just a fast, repeatable tap count.
